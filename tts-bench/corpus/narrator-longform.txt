Most conversations about AI in business start in the wrong place. They start with the tool, which model, which subscription, which vendor, and work backwards towards a problem it might solve. That order rarely survives contact with the work.

The businesses that win with AI won't be the ones with the biggest budgets. They'll be the ones who understood the problem before buying the tool.

There is a practical reason this matters more now than it did a year ago. A machine costing less than a mid-range car can run a capable language model entirely on your own premises, with no data leaving the building and no per-token bill arriving at the end of the month. We have been measuring one of these for the past few weeks, and the numbers are more interesting than the marketing.

It is not as fast as the frontier services, and for some tasks it is not as good. But for a great many everyday jobs, summarising a long document, drafting a first pass, pulling structure out of messy notes, the gap has narrowed to the point where capability is no longer the deciding factor. What decides it is whether the work involves information you would rather not send to somebody else's server.

That is a business question rather than a technical one. Which is usually where the useful conversation begins.
